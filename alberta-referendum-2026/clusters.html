Ridings that vote alike

Ten questions, one answer

Alberta didn't really vote on ten questions. It answered one question ten times. A riding that leaned Yes on immigration leaned Yes on the Senate, on judges and on the rest of the ballot. One measure, how strongly a riding votes Yes overall, accounts for 96% of the differences between ridings. Of the 76 ridings with most of their polls counted, 31 said Yes to all of the first nine questions and 12 said No to all nine.

That is why the first map sorts neatly into four bands. At one end are the city cores: about 39% Yes on the first nine questions, and 14% backed starting a separation referendum. At the other is rural central Alberta, at 75% and 44%. Even there, the referendum option mostly falls short: only two ridings, Rimbey-Rocky Mountain House-Sundre (51.1%) and Central Peace-Notley (50.7%), narrowly back a referendum. Province-wide it sits at 24.8%.

Look past each riding's overall lean and two questions stand out. The first is provincial control of immigration. Ridings in northeast Calgary and parts of south Edmonton supported it more than their other votes would suggest. Central Edmonton supported it less. The second is the separation question. Calgary-East, Edmonton-Highlands-Norwood and several rural ridings in the north and south were more open to a referendum than the rest of their ballot would suggest.

A few cautions. This analysis is based on partial results: 4,841 of 5,583 polls reported, as observed at 1:52 p.m. MDT on October 20, 2026. Figures will change as the count finishes. These issue groupings are loose tendencies, not fixed camps. And riding results describe places, not how any individual voted.

How we did this

The results come from CBC News's live results feed for all 87 ridings, using Elections Alberta's 2019 riding boundaries. For the grouping, we used results as observed at 1:52 p.m. MDT on October 20, 2026, when 4,841 of 5,583 polls had reported. For each riding, we took the share of Yes votes on each of the 10 questions (Referendum on Question 10) and split it into two parts: the riding's overall leaning, meaning how strongly it voted Yes across the whole ballot, and its issue profile, meaning the questions where it voted more or less Yes than that leaning would predict. A standard clustering method (k-means) then sorted ridings with similar numbers into groups. To test whether the groups were real, we reran the grouping 200 times on reshuffled samples of ridings. The overall-leaning groups held up well in these repeated tests (a stability score of 0.85 out of 1); the issue-profile groups less so (0.61), which is why we call them exploratory. Ridings with fewer than half their polls counted were left out of the grouping and placed afterward; they are hatched on the map. To explain the groups, we trained a model to reproduce them and measured how much each question mattered to its decisions (a technique called SHAP), repeating the check 30 times. These are riding-level patterns. They describe places, not how individuals voted.
