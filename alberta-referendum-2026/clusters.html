Ridings that vote alike

Same lean, different priorities

Most of the differences between Alberta's ridings come down to one thing: how strongly they voted Yes overall. A riding that leaned Yes on immigration also leaned Yes on the Senate, on judges and on the rest of the ballot. That overall leaning accounts for 96% of the differences between ridings, and it largely separates the cities from the countryside.

This page sets that leaning aside. For each riding, it asks which questions drew more or less Yes than the riding's overall leaning would predict. Grouping ridings by that pattern gives three clusters, and each one mixes city, suburban and rural ridings.

The separation question does the most sorting. Lesser Slave Lake, Peace River, Cardston-Siksika and other rural ridings in the north and south, along with Edmonton-Highlands-Norwood, were more open to a referendum than the rest of their ballot would suggest. A second group, led by ridings in northeast Calgary, leaned more Yes on provincial control of immigration. A third group, including central Edmonton ridings such as Edmonton-City Centre and Edmonton-Riverview, leaned less Yes on limiting provincial programs by immigration status. No single question stands out the way the overall leaning does.

This analysis uses partial results observed at 8:24 p.m. Mountain time on October 20, 2026, when 5,101 of 5,583 polls had reported. In many ridings the later questions had not been counted as fully as the first. The groups are therefore built from the 45 ridings where every question was close to fully counted. The other 42 are placed provisionally and hatched on the map. The figures will change as the count finishes, and riding results describe places, not how any individual voted.

How we did this

The results come from CBC News's live results feed for all 87 ridings. The maps use Elections Alberta's 2019 riding boundaries. The grouping uses results observed at 8:24 p.m. Mountain time on October 20, 2026, when 5,101 of 5,583 polls had reported.

For each riding, we took the share of Yes votes on each question, counting Referendum as Yes on Question 10. We split these into two measures. A riding's overall leaning is how strongly it voted Yes across the whole ballot. Its issue profile is a list of ten numbers, one per question, showing how much more or less Yes it voted on that question than its leaning predicts. We set the overall leaning aside and grouped ridings by issue profile alone. To compare two ridings, we used cosine similarity, which measures how closely their two lists point in the same direction. It is 1 when two ridings depart from their leaning on the same questions in the same proportions, 0 when their patterns are unrelated, and −1 when they are opposite. It ignores how large the departures are, so a riding with strong departures can match one with mild departures of the same shape. A standard clustering method, k-means, then sorted ridings with similar patterns into groups.

To check whether the groups were stable, we reran the grouping 200 times on reshuffled samples of ridings. The groups held up well, with a stability score of 0.86 out of 1. Three kinds of ridings were left out of the grouping and placed afterward. Some had fewer than half their polls counted. In others, some question had less than 80% of its usual vote count, because later questions are often counted after the first. A few departed so little from their overall leaning that their pattern could not be told apart from counting noise. All three kinds are hatched on the map.

To explain the groups, we trained a model to reproduce them. We then measured how much each question mattered to its decisions, using a technique called SHAP, and repeated this check 30 times. All of these patterns describe ridings, not how any individual voted.
