Ridings that vote alike

Ten questions, one answer

Alberta didn't really vote on ten questions. It answered one question ten times. A riding that leaned Yes on immigration also leaned Yes on the Senate, on judges and on the rest of the ballot. A single measure, how strongly a riding votes Yes overall, accounts for 96% of the differences between ridings. Of the 76 ridings with most of their polls counted, 31 voted Yes on all of the first nine questions and 12 voted No on all nine.

So the first map falls into four bands. At one end are the city cores. They voted about 39% Yes on the first nine questions, and 14% backed starting a separation referendum. At the other end, rural central Alberta voted 75% Yes on the first nine and 44% for a referendum. Even there, the referendum option mostly fell short. Only two ridings backed it, both narrowly: Rimbey-Rocky Mountain House-Sundre (51.1%) and Central Peace-Notley (50.7%). Province-wide it sits at 24.8%.

Set aside each riding's overall lean and two questions stand out. The first is provincial control of immigration. Ridings in northeast Calgary and parts of south Edmonton supported it more than their other votes would suggest, and central Edmonton supported it less. The second is the separation question. Calgary-East, Edmonton-Highlands-Norwood and several rural ridings in the north and south were more open to a referendum than the rest of their ballot would suggest.

This analysis uses partial results observed at 1:52 p.m. Mountain time on October 20, 2026, when 4,841 of 5,583 polls had reported. The figures will change as the count finishes. The issue groupings are loose tendencies, and riding results describe places, not how any individual voted.

How we did this

The results come from CBC News's live results feed for all 87 ridings. The maps use Elections Alberta's 2019 riding boundaries. The grouping uses results observed at 1:52 p.m. Mountain time on October 20, 2026, when 4,841 of 5,583 polls had reported.

For each riding, we took the share of Yes votes on each question, counting Referendum as Yes on Question 10. We then split each riding's votes into two measures. Its overall leaning is how strongly it voted Yes across the whole ballot. Its issue profile shows the questions where it voted more or less Yes than that leaning predicts. A standard clustering method, k-means, sorted ridings with similar numbers into groups.

To check whether the groups were stable, we reran the grouping 200 times on reshuffled samples of ridings. The overall-leaning groups held up well, with a stability score of 0.85 out of 1. The issue-profile groups scored 0.61, so we label them exploratory. Ridings with fewer than half their polls counted were left out of the grouping and placed afterward. They are hatched on the map.

To explain the groups, we trained a model to reproduce them. We then measured how much each question mattered to its decisions, using a technique called SHAP, and repeated this check 30 times. All of these patterns describe ridings, not how any individual voted.
