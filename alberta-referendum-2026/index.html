Alberta referendum 2026 – results by riding

What Alberta said

Alberta is staying put. CBC News projects that a majority of Albertans voted to remain in Canada. About three in four voters chose Remain on Question 10. They rejected starting the legal process toward a binding referendum on separation.

The other nine questions were much closer. They covered immigration, social programs, voter ID, judges, the Senate and federal powers. Most are within ten points of an even split, and the rest of the count could still change the result on one or two. Requiring proof of citizenship to vote is the clear exception: nearly two in three voters supported it.

Where a riding sits mattered more than which question was asked. Rural central Alberta leaned Yes on almost every question. The cores of Edmonton and Calgary leaned No on almost every question. The ridings that vote alike page maps these patterns and the few questions where ridings broke from them.

This summary uses partial results observed at about 2:30 p.m. Mountain time on October 20, 2026, when 4,887 of 5,583 polls had reported. The list below and the maps update from CBC News's live feed every hour. All results are unofficial until Elections Alberta certifies them.

    How we did this

    The results come from CBC News's live results feed for all 87 ridings. The maps use Elections Alberta's 2019 riding boundaries. The grouping on the ridings that vote alike page uses results observed at 1:52 p.m. Mountain time on October 20, 2026, when 4,841 of 5,583 polls had reported.

    For each riding, we took the share of Yes votes on each question, counting Referendum as Yes on Question 10. We then split each riding's votes into two measures. Its overall leaning is how strongly it voted Yes across the whole ballot. Its issue profile shows the questions where it voted more or less Yes than that leaning predicts. A standard clustering method, k-means, sorted ridings with similar numbers into groups. For issue profiles, we compared the pattern across questions, not its size. Two ridings count as similar if they depart from their leaning on the same questions in the same direction, even if one departs much further than the other.

    To check whether the groups were stable, we reran the grouping 200 times on reshuffled samples of ridings. The overall-leaning groups held up well, with a stability score of 0.85 out of 1. The issue-profile groups scored 0.77, just under the 0.8 usually taken as stable, so we label them exploratory. Two kinds of ridings were left out of the grouping and placed afterward. Some had fewer than half their polls counted. Others departed so little from their overall leaning that their pattern could not be told apart from counting noise. That second rule applies only to the issue-profile grouping. Both kinds are hatched on the grouping map.

    To explain the groups, we trained a model to reproduce them. We then measured how much each question mattered to its decisions, using a technique called SHAP, and repeated this check 30 times. All of these patterns describe ridings, not how any individual voted.
